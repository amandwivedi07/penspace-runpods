## Fighting Unwanted Feelings Can Make Them Control More of Your Life

Suppose anxiety arrives before an important conversation. Your first reaction may be to get rid of it. You distract yourself, argue with the feeling, postpone the conversation, or wait for the moment when you finally feel calm enough to act. The relief from avoidance is immediate. Unfortunately, it also teaches the mind something: anxiety is dangerous, and escape is what keeps you safe.

Harris calls this pattern experiential avoidance—trying to escape, suppress, or control uncomfortable thoughts, feelings, memories, and physical sensations. Some avoidance is perfectly sensible. If a situation is genuinely harmful and can be changed, change it. The problem begins when life becomes organized around avoiding internal discomfort that cannot be permanently eliminated.

Consider someone who feels anxious in social situations. Declining an invitation reduces anxiety tonight. Leaving a gathering early brings relief. Avoiding unfamiliar people prevents embarrassment. Each decision makes sense in isolation, yet repeated over months or years, the person’s world may become smaller. The strategy works beautifully at reducing discomfort in the short term while failing at the larger goal of building connection.

ACT offers acceptance as an alternative. Acceptance does not mean liking pain, resigning yourself to harmful circumstances, or deciding that nothing should change. It means allowing an unwanted internal experience to be present when fighting it would cost more than carrying it.

Harris often describes this as making room. Instead of tightening around anxiety, sadness, or anger, notice where the feeling appears in the body. Breathe around it. Observe its shape, movement, temperature, or intensity without demanding that it disappear. The purpose is not secretly to perform acceptance so cleverly that the emotion finally goes away. If disappearance remains the goal, the struggle has simply changed tactics.

This can feel counterintuitive because discomfort seems to demand action. But emotions rise, shift, and fall naturally when we stop turning their presence into an emergency.

Acceptance becomes meaningful because of what it allows next. You can feel nervous and still speak. You can carry grief while remaining connected to people you love. You can experience disappointment without abandoning something important.

The central question becomes: Is avoiding this feeling helping me build the life I want?

Sometimes the answer will be yes; danger should be avoided. But often what we are fleeing is not danger. It is the emotional price of doing something that matters.

Freedom does not require feeling comfortable. It requires becoming less controlled by the need to be comfortable.

The Present Moment Is Where Life Happens, but the Mind Is Constantly Pulling Us Elsewhere

You can spend dinner replaying something that happened at work. You can walk through a beautiful place while mentally rehearsing tomorrow’s problems. You can sit with someone you love while worrying about a future that has not happened. The body remains in one place while attention lives somewhere else.

Harris calls the ability to return deliberately to what is happening now contact with the present moment. ACT does not claim that thinking about the past or future is bad. Remembering helps us learn; planning helps us prepare. The problem is losing flexibility—becoming so absorbed in memory, prediction, judgment, or worry that we cannot notice the life occurring around us.

This is especially common when difficult emotions appear. Anxiety pulls attention toward possible future threats. Regret pulls it toward events that cannot be changed. The mind behaves as though enough analysis might finally make uncertainty disappear or rewrite the past. Meanwhile, the present becomes background scenery.

Harris uses simple mindfulness practices to strengthen the ability to come back. Notice the breath. Feel your feet on the floor. Pay attention to sounds in the room. Observe what you can see without immediately judging it. During a conversation, return attention to the person speaking whenever the mind wanders into preparation, worry, or self-consciousness.

The purpose is not to achieve a permanently tranquil state. Mindfulness can become another happiness trap if we use it only as a method for making unpleasant feelings disappear. Harris’s version is more practical: attention is something we can repeatedly redirect toward what is useful now.

This matters because values can only be lived in the present. You cannot be caring yesterday or courageous next month. You can remember caring moments and plan courageous actions, but actual behavior happens now. If attention is completely captured by internal commentary, we may miss opportunities to act like the person we want to be.

Present-moment awareness also makes psychological pain easier to separate from the larger environment. You might notice anxiety in your chest while also noticing sunlight through a window, the voice of someone beside you, and the fact that you are still capable of choosing your next action. The anxiety remains real without becoming the whole experience.

ACT therefore does not ask us to escape thought and live in some permanently pure present. Minds travel through time because that ability is useful.

The skill is being able to return when returning matters.

Life contains memories and possibilities, but the only place where we can actually respond to it is the moment we are already in.
